Project Overview

This research investigates whether structural geometries inspired by biological forms may influence the strength-to-weight performance of fabricated column specimens. The study involves CAD design, specimen fabrication, controlled mechanical testing, data collection, and comparative analysis.

  • Develop and fabricate column specimens incorporating selected biomimetic cellular geometries.

Research Motivation

2. Research Rationale

Columns are structural components designed to transfer loads. Their performance depends on material properties, geometry, slenderness, cross-sectional characteristics, fabrication, and loading conditions. Because geometry is one of the factors a designer can change directly, load-bearing columns are a suitable focus for investigating how structural arrangement relates to measured performance.

A controlled, small-scale setting allows specimens to be fabricated and tested under consistent conditions, so that differences between designs can be compared with fewer confounding factors than a full-scale study would involve.

3. Why Biomimetic Design?

Biological structures provide potential design references because many organisms have skeletal or shell architectures that serve structural functions. This research does not assume that biological structures are inherently superior. It investigates whether specific geometries inspired by biological forms show measurable differences under the conditions of this experiment.

4. Research Question

RESEARCH QUESTION“To what extent do biomimetic cellular structures improve the strength-to-weight performance of load-bearing column models compared with a conventional structural design?”

Key terms

  • Biomimetic design: design that draws on forms or organizational principles observed in biological systems.
  • Cellular geometry: an internal arrangement of repeated geometric units, with voids between solid walls.
  • Load-bearing column: a vertical member that carries and transfers compressive load.
  • Mechanical performance: the measured response of a specimen under test. Define the exact measurement used in your procedure.
  • Strength-to-weight performance: measured mechanical performance considered relative to specimen mass.

5. Research Objective

The objective is to experimentally compare structural designs and examine the relationship between measured mechanical performance and specimen mass.

Literature Review

1. Biomimicry in Structural Engineering

Biomimicry applies observations of biological form and function to engineering problems. Biological structural arrangements have been studied as references for engineered components. Observing a structure in nature is not the same as validating it in engineering: a biological form must be reinterpreted, fabricated in a chosen material, and tested before any performance claim can be made.

2. Cellular Structures

Cellular structures consist of repeated geometric units that enclose voids. The internal organization may influence load distribution, deformation, stability, and the amount of material used. Differences in unit shape, wall thickness, and arrangement can change how a component responds to load.

3. Biological Structural Models

A. Honeycomb-Inspired Structures

Honeycomb arrangements of repeated hexagonal cells have been widely studied in structural and materials engineering as a way to arrange material around enclosed space.

B. Lobster Shell-Inspired Structures

Lobster exoskeletons are studied for their organization, including layering and geometry within the shell. These features are a potential source of inspiration for engineered architectures.

C. Sea Urchin-Inspired Structures

Sea urchin skeletons have an organized geometric arrangement that has been considered as inspiration for structural design.

The fabricated specimens in this study are engineering interpretations inspired by these biological structures. They are not claimed to replicate the biology of the organisms exactly.

4. Load-Bearing Columns

Columns transfer structural loads, typically in axial compression. Behavior is influenced by:

  • cross-sectional geometry
  • material properties
  • specimen dimensions
  • slenderness
  • axial loading
  • buckling
  • local deformation
  • fabrication variability

Material failure occurs when the material itself cannot sustain the stress. Structural instability, such as buckling, is a geometric response in which a member loses stability, which can occur before the material reaches its own limit. Which of these governs depends on the specimen and the test.

5. Strength-to-Weight Performance

Evaluating performance relative to mass matters in structural engineering because a heavier member can often carry more load simply by containing more material. Comparing designs fairly requires consistent conditions, including material, fabrication settings, and test method.
